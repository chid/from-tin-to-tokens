Part VIII: The Hardware Landscape

Specialised Silicon

The GPU’s generality tax

A GPU can run any kernel, and that generality costs die area and energy. Scheduling hardware for divergent work, a multi-level cache hierarchy for unpredictable access patterns, and on consumerderived parts a graphics pipeline inference never touches. Specialised inference silicon removes what it doesn’t need and spends the area on what it does.

The trade is always flexibility for efficiency, and the interesting question is never whether it’s faster. It’s how much of the software stack you have to rebuild, and what happens when the model architecture changes.

Google TPU, the systolic array at scale

The TPU is the most commercially significant non-GPU AI accelerator, and its absence from most GPUcentric treatments is a real gap.

Architecturally it’s a systolic array , a large 2-D grid of multiply-accumulate units through which operands flow, so a matrix multiply streams data through the array with each element reused many times without returning to memory. Fewer control units, no cache hierarchy in the GPU sense, very high MAC density per unit of area and power, with large on-chip memories and a vector unit for the non-matmul work sitting around the array.

The seventh generation, Ironwood , is explicitly inference-first, with 192 GB of HBM3E per chip at about 7.4 TB/s, 4,614 TFLOPS of FP8 and 2,307 BF16, native FP8 support for the first time in the line, and pods scaling to 9,216 chips connected by Google’s inter-chip interconnect in a 3-D torus.

Three things are architecturally distinctive for inference. The pod is the unit , since the interconnect is designed for the whole pod to behave as one machine, which changes what distributed inference means because parallelism strategies assume a torus rather than a fat tree. The compiler is the runtime , since XLA compiles the whole model graph including collectives and schedules memory movement statically, so there’s no equivalent of writing your own kernel and dispatching it, which is enormously productive when your model fits the compiler’s assumptions and painful when it doesn’t. And shapes are static , since compilation is per-shape, so dynamic serving workloads need bucketing, which interacts awkwardly with continuous batching’s ragged batches and is where TPU serving stacks invest heavily.

There’s a lesson here for a GPU-focused engineer. Much of what this book calls optimisation, fusing kernels and removing launch overhead and scheduling memory movement, is what a good compiler does automatically on a machine designed for it. The GPU’s flexibility is why we do it by hand.

Groq, deterministic dataflow

Groq’s LPU takes determinism as the organising principle. There’s no HBM , just roughly 230 MB of SRAM per chip with up to about 80 TB/s of on-die bandwidth, so every byte is on chip. There’s no dynamic scheduling , since the compiler assigns every operation to a cycle, so there are no cache

misses, no arbitration, and no variability. Which gives deterministic latency , known at compile time, a genuinely different product property rather than a benchmark number.

The consequence is exceptional single-stream latency, because with weights in SRAM at tens of TB/s the memory wall that defines GPU decode simply isn’t there.

The costs are that model size is bounded by aggregate SRAM, so a large model spans many chips and the interconnect becomes the constraint, the schedule is compiled for a configuration so dynamic batching in the GPU sense doesn’t exist, and every model is a compilation rather than a checkpoint load.

Cerebras, wafer-scale

Cerebras integrates at wafer scale, and the WSE-3 carries 4 trillion transistors, about 900,000 cores, and 44 GB of on-chip SRAM with roughly 21 PB/s of memory bandwidth , three orders of magnitude beyond HBM.

The inference implication is direct. If weights are in SRAM, decode isn’t memory-bound in any sense this book would recognise, and per-user token rates go far beyond GPU systems. Models too large for onwafer SRAM stream weights from external memory, which reintroduces a bandwidth constraint at a different point.

Costs are extreme capital cost, a proprietary and less mature software stack, and the same compile-permodel workflow.

SambaNova and reconfigurable dataflow

SambaNova’s reconfigurable dataflow architecture sits between the GPU’s runtime flexibility and the compiled dataflow machines, since the on-chip dataflow can be reconfigured at load time rather than only at compile time, with a three-tier memory hierarchy of on-chip SRAM, on-package HBM, and external DRAM whose placement the compiler manages. The pitch is fast switching between many models on one system.

How to evaluate a new accelerator

A checklist that survives contact with vendor benchmarks.

Start with bytes per token , asking where weights live and where KV lives and what the achievable bandwidth to each is, because that predicts decode speed better than any FLOPS number. Then ask where the KV cache goes , since many accelerators are excellent at weights and have nowhere sensible to put a growing per-sequence cache, so ask about 64K context rather than batch-1 shortprompt throughput. Ask what the batching model is , because limited dynamic batching means throughput under real arrival patterns falls far below the benchmark. Ask what compile time and model support look like , since a four-week turnaround for a new architecture is a business constraint rather than a technical detail. Ask what the SLO distribution looks like , because deterministic hardware has an unusually good tail that may be worth more than mean throughput. And ask what you have to rebuild , since the scheduler, KV manager, sampler, structured output, and observability of Parts IV and IX are often assumed to exist and often don’t.

Key Concept

Specialised silicon trades flexibility for efficiency. TPUs use systolic arrays with a compiler-as-runtime model and pod-scale interconnect, and are the most significant non-GPU platform. Groq eliminates the memory hierarchy for deterministic latency, and Cerebras puts 44 GB of SRAM on a wafer at around 21 PB/s. Evaluate any of them on bytes per token, where the KV cache lives, the batching model, and how much of the serving stack you have to rebuild, rather than on peak FLOPS.
