Preface

Why we wrote this

Almost everything genuinely known about serving large models quickly lives inside a small number of companies, and very little of it gets written down. The papers describe the algorithms beautifully and stop at the point where the engineering starts. The blog posts announce results without the arithmetic underneath them. And the parts that cost the most to learn, which offsets the kernel argument buffer actually sits at, why your collective is fast in testing and wrong under load, which of your buffers quietly ended up in host memory, are passed around as folklore between people who have already been burnt.

We wrote this because we had to learn all of it, and because writing it down turned out to be the only way to be sure we had.

At Maincode we build frontier AI in Australia. That means training our own models and running them on our own machines, and it means the inference layer isn’t something we buy and forget about. It’s the layer where the physics of the hardware meets the economics of the product, and it’s where most of the difference between a demo and a business gets decided. Everything in this book came out of building that layer, on real silicon, with the benchmarks pointed at us.

On Australian-made AI

There’s a version of the next decade where a handful of companies in two countries build all of the frontier systems and everyone else consumes them through an API. We don’t think that’s a good outcome for anyone, including the people building those systems, and we’re not especially interested in participating in it from the consuming end.

Australian-made AI isn’t about flags on datacentres. It’s about whether the people here can build the thing rather than only use it, whether they understand it deeply enough to make it better, and whether the knowledge stays somewhere it can be taught. Australia has the researchers, the power, the land, and increasingly the silicon. What it has needed is more people who have actually taken a stack apart and put it back together, which is a thing you learn by doing and then, ideally, by telling someone.

That’s the argument for this book being free and detailed rather than a case study with the numbers taken out.

Why industry has to contribute

Frontier systems design is not a field you can advance from the outside. Some of it can be reasoned about on paper, and the rest of it only shows up when you have eight real accelerators, a real model, real traffic, and a benchmark you’re allowed to lose.

You can’t discover from a paper that three structurally different rewrites of a kernel all plateau at exactly the same number because a scratch buffer ended up in host memory. You can’t derive from first principles that remote writes stream almost twice as well as remote reads on a given fabric, or that

cache-bypassing stores make things worse on the part you actually have. Those are measurements, and only the people with the machines can take them.

Which puts an obligation on those people. Industry has the hardware and the scale, so industry has the observations, and an observation that never leaves the building isn’t advancing anything. The research community has been generous with this field, and the systems side of it has often been less so. This book is a contribution in the other direction. Every number in it states its conditions, every measurement names its hardware, and the places where our stack loses to a mature library are printed alongside the places where it wins, because a result you can’t check isn’t a result.

If you find something wrong in here, and you will, that’s the most useful thing you can send back.

Acknowledgements

A book like this reads as though one person did all of it, which is an artefact of how books get written rather than how anything gets built.

Every measured number in here needed a machine that existed. Racked, powered, cooled, cabled, and working on the particular morning somebody wanted to run a benchmark on it. The sustained bandwidth ceiling in Chapter 4, the all-reduce table in Chapter 28, the per-step correctness results in Chapter 36, none of those are things I could have produced alone, because none of them are results at all without eight accelerators sitting in a rack that other people built and keep alive.

That work doesn’t show up in a commit log. It shows up here as the thing I got to take for granted, and being able to take the machine for granted is the entire reason there was room to go this deep into one layer of it.

Maincode isn’t a group of people who occasionally hand each other things. The cluster, the infrastructure, the models, the product, the operations, the commercial side, all of it runs because everyone does their own part every day, and any one of those parts stopping would have stopped this too. I’d rather say that plainly than itemise who did what, because splitting it into contributions would misrepresent how it actually works.
