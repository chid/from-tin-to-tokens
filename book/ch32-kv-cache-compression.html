Part VII: Quantisation and Compression

KV Cache Compression

Beyond quantisation

Chapter 11 reduced the bytes per KV entry. This chapter reduces the number of entries, or avoids storing some of them at all, through eviction of entries that contribute little, merging of similar entries, cross-layer sharing where one layer’s KV serves several, and offloading of cold entries to a slower tier rather than discarding them.

These are orthogonal to quantisation and compose with it. They’re also orthogonal to the sparse attention of Chapter 14, which reduces what gets read without reducing what gets stored, and getting that distinction right matters. Sparsity buys speed, compression buys capacity, quantisation buys both.

Attention sinks, and why they exist

One finding constrains every eviction policy in this chapter. The first few tokens of a sequence receive disproportionate attention regardless of their content (Xiao et al., 2023), and evicting them collapses generation immediately rather than gradually.

The explanation has two parts and you need both. Softmax must sum to one , so when a query has no strong affinity for any key the model still has to put its attention mass somewhere, meaning it needs a dump. And causal masking makes the earliest tokens universally visible , since the first token is the only key every query can attend to, which during training makes it the natural place to dump excess attention, and the model learns to use it.

So attention sinks are a structural artefact of the architecture rather than a property of the text, and that’s why StreamingLLM’s remedy is so cheap. Keep the first four or so tokens permanently plus a rolling window of recent tokens and generation stays stable to arbitrary length, where a pure rolling window collapses as soon as the sinks fall out.

Any eviction policy has to protect the sinks. One that doesn’t will work in testing on short sequences and fail catastrophically in production on long ones.

H2O and score-based eviction

H2O (Zhang et al., 2023) formalises the observation that attention is concentrated, with a small set of heavy-hitter tokens accumulating most of the attention mass while the rest contribute little. It maintains a fixed KV budget and evicts the tokens with the lowest accumulated attention scores, keeping heavy hitters and recent tokens.

Quality depends strongly on the budget, near-lossless at high retention and degrading as the budget tightens, and degrading non-uniformly , so tasks needing precise recall of specific earlier tokens, retrieval and long-document QA and code editing, fail long before summarisation does.

The mechanism has a structural cost worth understanding. Computing eviction scores requires the attention weights, which a FlashAttention-style kernel never materialises, so either you produce them at extra cost in work and memory or you approximate them, and the approximation quality bounds the policy’s quality.

Merging and cross-layer sharing

Merging combines entries rather than discarding them. CaM merges to-be-evicted value states into retained ones so their contribution isn’t lost outright, KVMerger exploits high token-level similarity among key states within a sequence, and D2O merges both keys and values using cosine-similarity weighting with an EMA threshold. Note that “token merging (Bolya et al.)” is sometimes cited here in error. That work is ToMe, which merges tokens in vision transformers and is an entirely different thing.

Cross-layer sharing exploits a different redundancy, that KV states in adjacent layers are often highly similar. MiniCache merges KV across consecutive layers from the middle of the model outward, with a retention mechanism for tokens that don’t merge well. Architecturally, models are also being designed with cross-layer KV sharing built in, which is a cleaner solution than recovering it after the fact.

All merging methods share a trade-off. They’re lossy in ways that are hard to bound analytically, they add per-step computation, and their benefit is highly architecture- and task-dependent.

Adaptive precision

The most deployable member of this family, because it’s nearly free to implement on top of a paged cache, is to vary precision by age . Recent tokens in FP8 or BF16, older tokens in FP4 or INT4.

The justification is empirical, that recent tokens receive more attention mass on average, and the implementation is a per-block precision tag in the block table, with the attention kernel dispatching to the right dequantisation path per block, which costs a branch per block rather than per element.

That’s the most reliable way to get most of four-bit’s capacity benefit at close to FP8’s quality, and it needs none of the score computation or merging machinery above.

Offloading rather than discarding

If the cache exceeds VRAM, the alternative to compression is a memory hierarchy, hot blocks in HBM, warm in host RAM over PCIe, cold on NVMe or a cluster KV store (Chapter 30).

The trade is explicit and computable. Fetching a block costs bytes over link bandwidth, recomputing it costs its tokens over the prefill rate, and discarding it costs quality. For a shared prefix many requests will use, fetching wins easily, and for a one-off suffix recomputation is usually cheaper.

Offloading has a decisive advantage over lossy compression for production use, which is that it’s exact . No quality risk to evaluate, no task-dependent failure mode, no need to re-validate when the model changes. Where the bandwidth exists, prefer it.

The deployability caveat

Lossy KV compression is research-active and carries genuine deployment risk, for three reasons that compound.

Attention patterns vary by model, prompt, and task, so a policy tuned on one distribution can fail on another. Degradation is non-uniform , so aggregate benchmark scores can hold steady while a specific capability, exact recall or long-range coreference or instruction adherence deep in a context, collapses. Averages hide exactly the failures that matter. And evaluation has to be adversarial , since needle-in-a-

haystack tests at multiple depths, multi-hop retrieval, and tasks requiring verbatim reproduction will surface problems that perplexity and standard benchmarks won’t.

The recommended order of adoption runs from safest to riskiest. Quantisation (Chapter 11), then sparse attention with a trained or well-validated selector (Chapter 14), then offloading, then adaptive precision, then sink-protected eviction, then merging. Move down the list only when the level above is insufficient, and validate each step on your own traffic.

Key Concept

KV compression extends context beyond what memory allows and it’s riskier than quantisation. Attention sinks, the first few tokens that absorb excess attention because softmax must sum to one and causal masking makes them universally visible, must never be evicted. Score-based eviction, merging, and crosslayer sharing all trade quality for capacity in ways that are hard to bound, while adaptive precision by block age and exact offloading to a slower tier give most of the benefit with far less risk. Evaluate adversarially, because averages hide the failures that matter.

Exercises

  1. Explain why a rolling-window KV cache collapses without attention sinks and is stable with four of them retained. What does the model do with the sink tokens’ value vectors?

  2. A FlashAttention-style kernel never materialises attention weights. Design a cheap approximation to pertoken attention mass suitable for driving an eviction policy, and state its failure mode.

  3. Compare the cost of fetching a 16-token KV block from host memory over PCIe 5.0 with recomputing it at 20,000 tokens/s prefill, for a 94-layer model in FP8. At what block size does fetching win?

  4. Build: implement per-block adaptive precision in a paged KV cache, recent blocks FP8 and older blocks FP4, and measure both memory saving and accuracy on a needle-in-a-haystack test at several context depths.
