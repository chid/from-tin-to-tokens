Part VI: Multi-GPU and Distributed

Collectives for Inference

Why all-reduce matters here

Tensor-parallel decode runs two all-reduces per layer , one after the attention output projection and one after the FFN down projection, so a 94-layer model does 188 per token. At 20 µs each that’s 3.8 ms per token before any useful work is counted, and at 2 µs each it’s 0.4 ms.

The messages are small. Hidden size × dtype width × tokens in the batch, which for hidden 4,096 in FP16 at batch 1 is 8 KiB and at batch 64 is 512 KiB. That’s the small-message latency regime , where synchronisation and launch dominate and bandwidth barely registers, and it’s worth noting that collective libraries are tuned primarily for the training regime, tens to hundreds of megabytes, which is a different problem with different answers.

Small messages, fused reduce-scatter and all-gather

The algorithm that wins at small sizes has four properties. Each GPU owns one chunk of the output. Every GPU drives all its own links, with no central coordinator and no tree. All cross-GPU traffic is writes rather than reads. And the reduce-scatter and all-gather phases are fused , with no global barrier between them.

The fusion is the interesting part. In the conventional two-phase structure every GPU has to finish reduce-scatter before any begins all-gather, so the links idle at the barrier. If instead each GPU exclusively owns its chunk’s dataflow, its reduce-scatter and all-gather can chain on its own queue with the AQL barrier bit ordering them (Chapter 25), and only one host synchronisation ends the whole operation. GPU g broadcasts its finished chunk while GPU h is still reducing.

Making that work needed two mechanisms from earlier chapters, a kernarg ring , because chained unwaited dispatches need distinct kernarg slots, and a no-signal chain flag , because only the final packet needs a completion signal. With those, the fused scheme beat the unfused one at every size including 1 KiB, 20.5 µs against 24, which is why the unfused path became a degenerate-case fallback rather than an alternative.

Large messages, GPU-driven one-shot kernels

At large sizes the calculus inverts. Bandwidth dominates, and the way to get it is to stop returning to the host at all.

A one-shot collective is a single persistent kernel per GPU performing scatter, reduce, and all-gather internally, synchronising with peers through device-side barriers built on system-scope atomics over peer-mapped flags. The host launches it once and waits once.

That’s the architecture MSCCL++ established (Hwang et al., Microsoft Research and Azure, ASPLOS 2026, and frequently miscited). Rather than exposing a monolithic allReduce() , MSCCL++ provides GPU-initiated put, signal, and wait primitives from which algorithms get composed inside a kernel, and it reports substantial gains over RCCL at large sizes on MI300X-class hardware.

Implementing it surfaces one hazard worth stating explicitly. A device-side grid barrier requires every participating workgroup to be co-resident on the GPU, and if the launch geometry allows more workgroups than fit, the barrier deadlocks. In mainarch’s implementation a workgroup-size-64 configuration did exactly that, and the unguarded spin wedged the compute command processor hard enough that queues couldn’t be evicted, with recovery only on process exit. Two fixes, bound every device-side spin with a watchdog so it fails rather than hangs, and choose a launch geometry that’s provably co-resident, where a workgroup size of 96 was safe on this part.

Measured results, including the losses

mainarch against RCCL, 8×MI355X, f32 all-reduce.

SIZE MAINARCH RCCL VERDICT
1 KiB 20.5 µs 47.3 µs mainarch 2.3×
256 KiB 40.2 µs 58.9 µs mainarch 1.5×
1 MiB 33 µs 66 µs mainarch 2×
4 MiB 58 µs 60 µs roughly even
64 MiB 206 GB/s 362 GB/s RCCL 1.75×

The first four rows are latency and the last is bus bandwidth, because that’s what each regime is actually limited by. The crossover sits between 4 and 8 MiB.

The last row is the important one. A from-scratch stack built for the inference regime wins decisively where inference lives, every size from 1 KiB to about 4 MiB, which covers all of tensor-parallel decode, and loses at training-scale messages. The gap narrowed from 3.4× to 1.75× when the one-shot GPUdriven kernel replaced the staged path, and it didn’t close.

The diagnosis is specific and it generalises. The two-shot algorithm moves the same total volume, 1.75n, as RCCL’s ring, but sustains only about 15 GB/s per link where RCCL sustains far more. What’s left is per-link write saturation, since RCCL’s pipelined ring keeps each link continuously busy with a stream of in-flight chunks while a two-shot scheme alternates phases and never gets past the fabric’s ramp-up from Chapter 27. Closing it needs multi-channel per-link streaming or a fully pipelined persistent kernel.

Reporting the loss isn’t modesty, it’s the only way the rest of the table means anything. A benchmark table with no losses in it has been filtered.

One more measured result worth keeping, because it contradicts a common intuition. Non-temporal, cache-bypassing stores did not help , taking 64 MiB throughput from 104 down to 96 GB/s, because on this part the cached path write-combines better. Cache-bypass hints are a hypothesis to test rather than an optimisation to apply.

Algorithm selection

MESSAGE SIZE ALGORITHM RATIONALE
≤ ~8 MiB Fused RS/AG, host-driven, chained Latency dominates, fusion removes the barrier
≥ ~16 MiB One-shot GPU-driven persistent kernel Bandwidth dominates, keep coordination on device

The crossover is fabric- and implementation-dependent and should be measured rather than assumed. A production implementation carries both and selects per call, and the selection logic itself has to be cheap, since it runs 188 times per token.

Beyond all-reduce

Inference needs more than all-reduce and the others have their own characters. All-gather and reducescatter as separate operations serve sequence parallelism, where the activation is sharded along the token dimension between them. All-to-all serves expert parallelism, with data-dependent sizes, load imbalance, and two invocations per layer, which makes it the hardest collective in inference and the one most in need of overlap. And point-to-point sends serve pipeline parallelism and KV transfer in disaggregated serving.

Overlapping communication with computation

The most valuable structural optimisation in this chapter isn’t a faster collective, it’s a collective that costs nothing because it happens underneath compute.

The standard technique decomposes a GEMM plus all-reduce into tiles, so as each output tile completes its contribution gets pushed to peers while the next tile computes. The dependency becomes per-tile rather than whole-operation, and communication and computation interleave. Variants show up as async tensor parallelism or fused GEMM-plus-collective kernels in every serious engine, and the gains are large because the two resources are otherwise idle in alternation.

The from-scratch version is the natural endpoint of owning both the GEMM and the collective, where the all-reduce isn’t a call after the GEMM, it’s the GEMM’s epilogue. That fusion is only expressible if you own both kernels, which is the strongest technical argument for building the compute path yourself rather than composing libraries.

Key Concept

Tensor-parallel decode runs two small all-reduces per layer, so small-message latency is what matters rather than bandwidth. Fuse reduce-scatter with all-gather so no barrier idles the links, drive all links from every GPU, and use writes. Measured against RCCL on 8×MI355X that wins by up to 2.3× from 1 KiB to about 4 MiB and loses by 1.75× at 64 MiB, because a two-shot scheme can’t saturate a link the way a pipelined ring can. Device-side barriers need co-resident launch geometry and watchdogs or they wedge the command processor. And the real prize is overlapping the collective with the GEMM that produces its input.

  2. Explain why fusing reduce-scatter with all-gather removes a barrier, and what ownership property of the data makes the fusion sound.

  3. The two-shot algorithm moves 1.75n bytes, the same as a ring, and sustains a fraction of the per-link bandwidth. Explain, then describe a multi-channel design that would close the gap.

  4. Build: implement a small-message all-reduce with peer writes and system-scope atomic flags. Verify bitexactness against a CPU reference, then measure against your vendor’s library from 1 KiB to 64 MiB and plot both curves. Report where you lose.
