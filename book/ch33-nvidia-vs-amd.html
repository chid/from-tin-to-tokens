Part VIII: The Hardware Landscape

Overview

Inference runs on more kinds of silicon every year. This part covers NVIDIA vs AMD, specialised silicon (Google TPU, Groq, Cerebras, SambaNova), and edge inference architectures (llama.cpp, MLC-LLM).

Part VIII: The Hardware Landscape

NVIDIA vs AMD

The axes that matter

Strip the marketing away and inference hardware differs on five things. Memory capacity per package, which decides whether the model fits and how much KV cache is left over. Memory bandwidth, which more or less directly decides decode speed. Low-precision compute throughput, which decides prefill speed and the ceiling on large-batch decode. Interconnect, which decides how large a coherent domain you can build and therefore the largest model you can serve with tight latency. And software maturity, which decides how much of the stack you have to build yourself.

The first four are on datasheets. The fifth decides most deployments.

NVIDIA’s advantage is that the stack is co-designed

CUDA is the dominant programming model with the largest developer base and by far the most documentation and answered questions. cuBLAS gives you production GEMM and CUTLASS gives you a composable library for writing your own, with an active tradition of publishing state-of-the-art kernel techniques. NCCL is mature and tuned over years across topologies. TensorRT-LLM is co-designed with each GPU generation, often with kernels for hardware features before they’re public. And Nsight Compute and Systems are the best GPU profiling tools available, where the gap is larger than any benchmark reveals because it compounds into every optimisation decision you make.

For most teams that’s the path of least resistance, and choosing it isn’t a failure of ambition.

AMD’s position

AMD’s hardware advantages are real and specific: more HBM per package with 288 GB on MI355X against 192 GB on B200, a very large last-level cache, and FP6 that runs at the FP4 rate. Blackwell supports FP4 and FP6 natively too, so the format itself is not the differentiator, but its FP6 runs at the FP8 rate, which leaves MI355X at roughly 10.1 dense PFLOPS of FP6 against B200’s 4.5. The software gap has narrowed substantially, since ROCm supports vLLM and SGLang and PyTorch as first-class targets, AITER and Composable Kernel provide tuned kernels, and hipBLASLt has closed much of the GEMM gap. Gaps remain in specific places.

The kernel ecosystem lags, since upstream FlashInfer is CUDA-only and AMD maintains a separate ROCm port that trails in features (Chapter 2), and bleeding-edge research kernels appear for CUDA first, sometimes by many months. Collectives are a port, since RCCL derives from NCCL, and it’s competent at large messages, beating a hand-written implementation above about 8 MiB as Chapter 28’s measurements show, while being weaker in the small-message latency regime decode actually uses. Profiling through ROCm’s profiler and rocprofv3 is functional and improving without matching Nsight Compute’s source-level attribution and guided analysis. And day-one model support lands on CUDA first.

Where AMD’s shape is decisively better is models that need capacity. A 235B MoE in FP8 is about 235 GB of weights, one byte per parameter, which fits on one 288 GB MI355X with roughly 50 GB left for KV

cache, and doesn’t fit on a 192 GB B200 or an 80 GB H100 at all. Serving it at long context needs fourbit weights, about 118 GB, before a large cache will sit beside them.

There’s a strategic argument for AMD in a from-scratch context that’s different in kind and worth naming, which is that the kernel ABI is documented and directly reachable . /dev/kfd is an ioctl interface with a published UAPI header, AQL is a published packet format, and the ISA documentation is public, while on NVIDIA the equivalent layer is undocumented and unstable. If your project’s premise is going below the runtime, that isn’t a detail, it’s the entire basis for the project being possible.

B300, Blackwell Ultra, raises HBM to 288 GB and NVFP4 throughput by roughly 50% over B200, which removes AMD’s capacity advantage at that tier.

Reading the table for inference, AMD leads on capacity per package and last-level cache while NVIDIA leads on interconnect bandwidth and, decisively, on the size of the coherent domain, since rack-scale NVLink systems put 72 GPUs in one domain, which is a different kind of machine rather than a faster one. For single-node serving of a large model the two are competitive on hardware and differ mainly on software, and for rack-scale serving of a very large MoE the domain size matters more than any per-chip number.

The 2026 roadmap

Both vendors are converging on the same shape, more HBM, more FP4, bigger coherent domains.

AMD’s MI400 series on CDNA 5 with the Helios rack brings 432 GB of HBM4 per GPU at 23.3 TB/s, up to 40.3 PFLOPS of MXFP4 and 20.1 of MXFP8 on the flagship MI455X, and a 72-GPU rack carrying around 31 TB of HBM4. NVIDIA’s Blackwell Ultra , B300 and GB300, and the Rubin generation following it continue the NVL rack-scale line.

The implication for anyone building a stack is uncomfortable and worth sitting with. HBM capacity is growing faster than bandwidth , which means the bytes-per-token pressure of this book’s decode

chapters gets relatively worse rather than better. More capacity means longer contexts and bigger caches, and bandwidth isn’t keeping pace, so everything in Chapters 10, 11, and 14 becomes more important over time.

When vendor choice actually matters

When capacity binds, since a model that fits on one package avoids tensor parallelism entirely and therefore avoids 188 all-reduces per token. When you need a kernel that exists on one side only, which is worth checking before committing. When you’re building below the runtime, where AMD’s documented kernel ABI decides it. When you need a rack-scale coherent domain, which is currently NVIDIA’s. And when cost per GB of HBM and per token decides procurement, which varies with market conditions and is frequently the actual deciding factor regardless of anything technical.

Key Concept

NVIDIA’s advantage is a co-designed stack and a profiling toolchain that compounds into every optimisation decision. AMD’s is capacity per package, a very large last-level cache, native FP4 and FP6, and, for a from-scratch builder, a documented kernel ABI that makes going below the runtime possible at all. Both roadmaps add HBM capacity faster than bandwidth, which makes the bytes-per-token discipline of this book more important over time rather than less.
